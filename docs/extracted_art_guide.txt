01 / WHAT WE WANT
Take these features from your pictures
From the robot image:
visible square pixels, worn metal and warm brick colors.
From Megabonk:
chunky shapes, clear colors and objects that are easy to recognize.
For your town: simple houses, broad roofs, chunky trees and slightly worn surfaces. Keep roads and grass quieter so enemies and bullets stand out.
The important part:
a “PSX shader” alone will not create this look. The shapes and painted surfaces do most of the work. Strong wobble and screen noise can stay off at first.
02 / THREE THINGS MAKE THE LOOK
Think of a house as a shape, a skin and lighting.
1. Model = the shape
The actual 3D house: walls, roof and chimney.
Use Blender.
Build it with a small number of simple shapes.
2. Texture = the skin
A flat image wrapped onto the house. It adds bricks, roof tiles and paint.
Use AI for a starting image, then a pixel editor such as Aseprite to clean it.
3. Material = how it looks in light
Godot combines the shape and texture, then adds light and shadow.
Use Godot.
Keep surfaces mostly matte, with visible pixel edges.
One useful term: UV mapping.
This tells the game where the texture belongs on the model—like wrapping paper around a box. Some of your current building-detail models need this added before images can wrap onto them correctly.
A shader is a small program that changes how Godot draws a surface. We can use one later to fine-tune the lighting and retro effects.
03 / WHAT TO DO FIRST
Build one small test area in five steps
Choose one example look.
Ask AI for a picture of your town. Choose a result whose shapes and colors you like.
Make one house, one road and one tree.
Build their 3D shapes in Blender. Give the house a clear roof shape because the helicopter sees it from above.
Make a few small texture images.
Start with brick, roof, grass and road. Try 128 × 128 pixels for each repeating surface. That is a starting size, not a rule for every object.
Put them in Godot.
Wrap the textures onto the models. Use “Nearest with Mipmaps” texture filtering: crisp pixels nearby, less flickering far away. Add simple sunlight.
Fly around and check.

01 / DECISION
The look comes from the assets first.
Working style name: “Chunky painted-pixel military town.”
Simple 3D silhouettes + deliberate low-resolution color textures + restrained lighting + coherent scale. Add PSX artifacts only after the town looks right without them.
Primary influence
Megabonk’s exaggerated shapes, large color areas and visibly textured surfaces. Make buildings, trees, vehicles and props easy to recognize while moving.
Surface influence
The robot reference’s square texels, warm masonry, worn painted metal and sharp geometric edges. Keep its surface character; reduce its dense noise for distant terrain.
Game identity
Your Nuclear Strike direction: town rooftops, roads, military installations, readable aircraft and destructible architecture, viewed from the existing elevated camera.
Use modern, stable perspective by default. Strong vertex wobble, warped textures, thick black outlines and full-screen dithering are separate effects; none is required to obtain the main look.
No image prompt can guarantee an exact, repeatable game-ready result. Prompting establishes direction; palette cleanup, UV work, modeling and in-game review produce consistency. Keep one approved asset sheet attached to later generations whenever your chosen generator supports references.

03 / THE STYLE CONTRACT
One visual language across the whole town
Shapes & proportions
Chunky roof volumes, thick eaves, broad window groups and substantial tree crowns.
Use geometry for silhouette and collision-relevant forms. Paint small seams, panel lines and chips.
Flat normals on masonry and angular props; selective smooth normals on rounded aircraft and trunks where useful.
Avoid making every object a uniformly beveled toy. Keep believable military and town proportions with simplified detail.

06 / TEXTURE DESIGN
Small textures need authored structure, not random noise.
Albedo
is the surface’s base color.
UVs
tell the mesh where to sample that image. A
texel
is one pixel of the texture. A
trim sheet
packs repeatable strips such as window frames and roof edges. An
atlas
packs several reusable image regions into one texture.
What belongs in the pixels
Large brick groups, uneven plaster patches, broad roof seams, chunky bark shapes, painted panel divisions and restrained edge wear. Use connected color clusters. Tiny random single-pixel marks usually turn into noise from the helicopter.
Paint subtle recess darkening; keep the main lighting neutral. A dramatic directional shadow painted into the texture remains fixed when the object turns and fights Godot’s sunlight.
What belongs in the mesh
Roof peaks, eaves, chimneys, overhangs, tree crowns, tank silhouettes, large frame edges and broken walls. If it changes the skyline or needs real collision, model it.
Do not model each brick, leaf or roof shingle. Use low-detail geometry with intentional UV allocation.
Nearest sampling · distinct square texels.
Linear sampling · blended texel boundaries.
Schematic browser illustration, not a generated game asset or an exact Godot lighting simulation. Low-resolution source art and filtering are two different decisions.
Choose density before image dimensions
At 16 texels/m, a 128-pixel tile covers 8 m. A 16 m wall uses that tile twice. A 256-pixel unique image spanning 16 m has the same density. A bigger file alone does not improve the style.

Prepare AI textures for actual use
Generate one flat material per image. Request orthographic, edge-to-edge, neutral light and no cast shadows. Ask for seamless repeats, but verify them yourself.
If the generator produces a large image, reduce it to the final working size. Compare area/box reduction for painted sources against nearest reduction for already aligned pixel art; then repaint the clusters. Always use nearest when enlarging the finished pixel texture for a preview.
Reduce and clean the palette in the pixel editor. Remove pseudo-text, gradients and stray single pixels. Exact dimensions and exact colors come from this editing step.
Offset by half the image width/height, repair the cross-shaped seam, then inspect a 3×3 repetition. Check both borders and repeated recognizable landmarks.
Assemble atlases in the editor against actual UV layouts. Start with 4–8 pixels of extruded gutters at base resolution and inspect distant mip levels; this is not a guarantee against all mip bleeding. Separate high-contrast regions or use separate repeating textures where needed.
Save PNG and inspect on the actual mesh under daylight, storm and night. Do not approve a texture just from its flat image.
A generated atlas is not automatically UV-ready.
Generated sheets can invent inconsistent panel sizes and perspectives. For unique props, unwrap the real model first and supply that UV layout as a locked guide if the tool supports it; finish the map in an editor. Keep UV islands aligned to whole texels where practical and consistent across destruction variants.

A post-process pixelation shader cannot add UVs, turn a smooth mesh into a good low-poly silhouette, or create painted material detail. Vertex snapping changes projected geometry; affine mapping changes texture interpolation. Neither is the same thing as low-resolution albedo.
Starter material shader · lit, textured, opaque
This example uses the same style of banded direct light as the project, now with an authored texture. It is a candidate for a new shared material, not a replacement pasted over every existing shader. Supply valid UVs and a texture. Vertex tint is optional to avoid multiplying an already colored texture by strong existing facade colors.
shader_type spatial;
render_mode diffuse_lambert, specular_disabled;
uniform sampler2D albedo_texture
: source_color, filter_nearest_mipmap, repeat_enable;
uniform vec4 tint : source_color = vec4(1.0);
uniform bool use_vertex_tint = false;
uniform vec2 uv_scale = vec2(1.0);
void fragment() {
vec3 base = texture(albedo_texture, UV * uv_scale).rgb;
vec3 vertex_tint = use_vertex_tint ? COLOR.rgb : vec3(1.0);
ALBEDO = base * tint.rgb * vertex_tint;
ROUGHNESS = 0.95;
METALLIC = 0.0;
}
void light() {
float n_dot_l = max(dot(NORMAL, LIGHT), 0.0);
float band = mix(0.35, 0.72,
smoothstep(0.12, 0.16, n_dot_l));
band = mix(band, 1.0,
smoothstep(0.62, 0.66, n_dot_l));
DIFFUSE_LIGHT += ALBEDO * LIGHT_COLOR * band
* ATTENUATION / 3.14159265;
}
Copy shader
The entire direct-light contribution is attenuated, preserving shadows. Ambient illumination is separate. This is a stylized lighting response, not an assertion about Megabonk’s shader. Disable forced vertex shading when testing a custom
light()
function. For an atlas, keep sampling inside assigned islands; this repeating sampler is intended for a whole repeating material tile.

Why wobble and outlines come later
Vertex snapping can cause visible movement at stationary geometry as the camera moves. It can also open cracks if adjoining meshes snap differently and can destabilize thin structures. Affine UV effects distort large polygons and need careful testing on roads and roofs. Neither should affect collision geometry.
If outlines help enemies separate from rooftops, prototype a selective inverted-hull mesh pass. It costs another draw and needs hard-edge/normal handling. A full-screen depth outline can also mark unwanted terrain edges. The normal/roughness screen buffer is unavailable in Compatibility, so do not copy a Forward+-only outline shader and assume it will work.

Reference block:
place one house, one shop, a road intersection, grass verge, two trees, a crate, a barrel, a parked vehicle and rubble in an isolated art review scene. Use the existing flight camera.
Material proof:
add UVs to one procedural surface or import one GLB; use the brick/roof/grass starter set. Compare standard diffuse lighting against the custom banded shader.
Destruction proof:
damage and collapse the house, break a prop, verify collision and material continuity.
Motion proof:
fly and turn at normal speed through day, storm and night. Check shimmer, shadow contrast, target visibility and distant texture bleeding.
Kit approval:
save comparison images at the same camera, lighting and resolution. Lock the palette, density and export conventions.
Scale out:
replace one district, measure performance with enemies and simultaneous destruction, then migrate the rest of the map.

12 / ACCEPTANCE
Approve the town from the pilot’s view.
Visual gate
House, shop, warehouse and fuel hazard are identifiable without zooming in.
Walls, roofs, trees, ground and rubble share a coherent texel scale and palette.
Material structure is visible; ground detail stays quieter than targets.
Damage states look like the same building, with correctly textured exposed interiors.
Roads, aircraft, projectiles and pickups remain distinct in grayscale as well as color.
Pixels remain deliberate; no blurred atlas edges or seams.
Runtime gate
Inspect while flying and turning, including grazing-angle roads and distant roofs.
No severe texture shimmer, z-fighting, drifting world-space textures on moving rubble, or clipping foliage.
Check daylight, night and storm; preserve target and HUD visibility.
Verify collision and navigation after destruction.
Measure frame time, draw calls, visible triangles and active physics debris under the existing enemy cap.
Compare against the current benchmark on the same device and resolution. If targeting 60 fps, 16.7 ms is the total frame budget—not an assumed asset budget.
The first approved house is the standard.
Save its model, textures, material, palette, camera screenshots and damage states together. All later town assets should match this reference set. That is how this direction stays consistent across future AI-assisted work.
